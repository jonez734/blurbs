# The Piddingtons (Sid and Lesley)

Sydney George Piddington (14 May 1918 – 29 January 1991) and Lesley Pope
Piddington (1925 – 2 August 2016) were an Australian husband-and-wife
mentalism team known professionally as **The Piddingtons**. Performing
between the late 1940s and the late 1960s, they are regarded as one of
the most famous stage and radio "telepathy" acts of the twentieth
century. The Piddingtons never revealed their working methods but also
never claimed to possess genuine paranormal powers — they were, in the
truest sense, mentalists.

## Background

Sydney Piddington spent part of World War II in [Changi Prisoner of War
camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Changi_Prison) after the fall of
Singapore. There he discovered that maintaining morale was essential
to prisoners' survival, and he teamed with the Australian writer and
fellow POW [Russell Braddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Braddon)
to develop a mentalism act for camp entertainments. It was at Changi
that Sydney developed many of the techniques that would later define
the Piddingtons' professional stage work.

Other notable inmates of Changi during this period included the
illustrator [Ronald Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Searle),
who filled notebooks with sketches of camp life that would later make
him famous, and the actor [John Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wood_(actor,_born_1909)),
with whom Sydney was repatriated to Australia after the war.

After returning home, Sydney was introduced to Lesley Pope at a
homecoming party for John Wood at Sydney's [Minerva
Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerva_Theatre,_Sydney), where
she was the leading lady in the current production. Sydney taught her
the techniques he had refined during the war, the two married, and
together they developed a full two-person telepathy stage act that
quickly became a popular attraction on Australian variety bills.

## The BBC Radio Broadcasts

In the early 1950s the Piddingtons relocated to London, where they were
signed to present a series of live mentalism demonstrations for the
[British Broadcasting Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC).
These broadcasts — designed to dramatize the apparent transmission of
thought over great distance — are the performances for which the act
is best remembered. Three in particular have survived in recordings
and are widely discussed today:

- **The Diving Bell Experiment.** Lesley was lowered into a diving bell
  in a swimming pool in [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey)
  while Sydney worked with a live studio audience at BBC headquarters
  in central London. Items and personal objects chosen by the audience
  were described by Lesley from underwater, with no obvious line of
  communication between them.
- **The Tower of London.** Lesley was held under armed guard inside
  the [Tower of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_London),
  on the opposite bank of the Thames from the BBC studios. She was
  still able to determine objects, names and short sentences chosen
  by audience members and by an independent panel of judges in the
  studio.
- **The Stratocruiser Broadcast.** Lesley was taken to cruising
  altitude aboard a [Boeing 377 Stratocruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_377_Stratocruiser)
  over a British military base. From the air she correctly identified
  the personal possessions of an audience member selected at random
  by the panel of judges on the ground.

## Methods: How It (Probably) Worked

The Piddingtons were officially billed as a "thought-transference" or
telepathy act but — a critical point that distinguishes them from
many of their contemporaries — they never publicly claimed to possess
genuine [ESP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasensory_perception).
Their working methods were a closely guarded secret, withheld even
from the BBC producers who engineered the broadcasts. After Sydney's
death in 1991 Lesley reportedly told her grandson, "Even if I wanted
to tell you how it was done, I don't think I would be able."

Magicians and psychologists who have studied the act over the decades
generally agree on three families of explanation:

1. **Concealed receivers and mechanical aids.** The experimental
   controls were notably weak. The Cambridge psychologist [Robert H.
   Thouless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Thouless), in
   his 1963 book *Experimental Psychical Research*, pointed out that
   in the Tower of London demonstration Lesley had produced a
   sentence that had already been read aloud on the radio to
   listeners — and that "no examination of Mrs Piddington's clothes
   or hair seems to have been made to ensure that she had not a
   wireless receiving set concealed on her person." Concealed
   radio receivers were a known technology of the era and would
   have allowed information to be relayed from the studio to Lesley
   wherever she was located.
2. **Prearrangement and code signals.** The dramatic settings
   themselves — underwater, in flight, in the Tower — may have
   served chiefly as misdirection, drawing attention away from
   what was actually being transmitted. The American magician and
   television personality [Penn Jillette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_Jillette)
   discussed two specific demonstrations in a 2015 follow-up to the
   2014 [Radiolab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiolab) episode
   "You Are The Judge." In the Stratocruiser broadcast, audience
   members wrote short messages, sealed them in numbered envelopes,
   and two envelopes were selected at random. Sydney then secretly
   swapped both envelopes with pre-prepared ones; because *two*
   envelopes were selected rather than one, each audience member
   assumed their own envelope was the one used, and the
   substitution went unnoticed. In the Diving Bell broadcast, the
   passage "chosen" by the studio audience was determined by a
   mathematical procedure that, in practice, always forced the
   same pre-selected passage — a classical [book test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_test)
   whose roots go back hundreds of years. Jillette's broader
   argument is that the entire dramatic apparatus of the
   broadcasts — the diving bell, the plane, the Tower — was
   itself the misdirection: while the audience hunts for some
   secret communication channel between husband and wife, no
   such channel is needed, because the outcomes have been
   arranged in advance.
3. **Confederates and accomplices.** The act's classic two-person
   structure necessarily involved at least one additional person
   (the receiver or "stooge") in the audience whose cooperation
   was essential to the routine. The wider BBC staff and stage
   crew may also have assisted in the more elaborate broadcasts
   without the studio audience's knowledge.

### The 2015 Book

In November 2015 the writer Martin Hart published *Piddington's
Secrets*, which claims to expose the act's working methods. Hart's
account is based on a notebook kept by his grandfather, who reportedly
worked on the BBC broadcasts and had the opportunity to observe the
act at close quarters. The Piddingtons' estate has not, as far as
public reporting goes, confirmed or denied the claims in the book.

## Legacy

The Piddingtons are widely cited as one of the greatest two-person
telepathy acts in the history of [mentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentalism).
They are routinely compared with [Julius and Agnes
Zancig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_and_Agnes_Zancig), the
other celebrated husband-and-wife telepathy team of the mid-twentieth
century, and their BBC broadcasts continue to be analysed and
performed by modern mentalists. The original BBC recordings have
been uploaded to YouTube in recent years and remain a popular
reference for anyone interested in the history of stage magic.

The act is also a recurring case study in the literature on the
skeptical evaluation of paranormal claims — most notably in Peter
Lamont's *Extraordinary Beliefs* (Cambridge University Press, 2013),
which discusses the Piddingtons alongside other twentieth-century
"psychic" performers whose methods were eventually reconstructed.
