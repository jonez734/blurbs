# Automan: The Holographic Hero

Automan (1983–1984) was a short-lived but memorable science fiction series created by Glen A. Larson — the same producer who gave us *Knight Rider*, *Battlestar Galactica*, and *Magnum, P.I.* The show featured an artificially intelligent computer construct as its hero, making it unique among 1980s television's AI portrayals [automan-1]. The series ran for 13 episodes (December 1983 – April 1984) on ABC before cancellation, but it left a memorable mark on 1980s pop culture and has developed a devoted cult following in the streaming era.

For a unified overview of AI across movies and TV from 1975 onward, see the [AI in Movies and TV](../ai-in-movies-and-tv) article. For the Tron visual-style influence on the show, see the [Tron blurb](../tron).

## The Concept

The series starred **Desi Arnaz Jr.** as Walter Nebicher, a police officer and computer-programming genius who was stuck behind a desk despite his skills. To prove himself, Walter created what he hoped would be "the ultimate crime-fighting program" — an artificially intelligent hologram that could take on criminals face-to-face [automan-1][automan-2].

Automan (played by **Chuck Wagner**) was designed as "the world's first automatic man" — an amalgam of all the most handsome men on the planet, made of solid light. Unlike traditional robots or androids, Automan was a holographic construct that could leave the computer world and manifest in the real one, provided there was enough electrical power to sustain him [automan-2].

Walter's senior officer, **Lt. Jack Curtis** (Robert Lansing), did not know about Automan. Walter's girlfriend, **Roxanne Caldwell** (Heather McNair), a reporter, learns the secret partway through the run. The show's central conceit is that the AI is more competent than the human police and that Walter has to keep the AI's existence secret from the people the AI is helping. Walter's exhaustion, frustration, and the slow realization that the AI is the better cop is the show's strongest character work, and the throughline that gives the program its 2020s cult following [automan-1].

## Cursor: The AI Sidekick

Automan's companion was **Cursor**, a floating, shifting polyhedron about the size of a softball that served as his sidekick and assistant. Cursor's most remarkable ability was "drawing" holographic objects from the computer world into reality — vehicles, weapons, and tools [automan-1]. Cursor is, technically, the more capable of the two AIs because he is the one that materializes the props; Automan is the one who uses them. Cursor has a small personality (a kind of loyal-jar energy) and is the comic relief.

The most commonly drawn objects were:

- **Autocar** — A holographic sports car based on a Lamborghini Countach LP400. In the fictional world, it could make 90-degree turns without losing control and could overtake by "strafing" rather than turning — exactly like a video game [automan-1][automan-2].
- **Autochopper** — A holographic helicopter based on a Bell JetRanger. Unlike real helicopters, it could land anywhere [automan-1].
- **Airplane** — A futuristic holographic aircraft [automan-1].
- **Motorcycle** — Additional vehicles appeared in various episodes [automan-1].
- **Weapons and tools** — Cursor could also generate handguns and other objects as needed [automan-1].

The special-effects team created these using black props with reflective tape — wireframe effects that deliberately emulated the visual style of Disney's *Tron* (1982) [automan-1]. The CGI costuming, the black backgrounds with bright line graphics, the recurring "automan tracer" effect (Automan's path, drawn in light as he moves), and the floating Cursor are all direct visual quotes from *Tron*. The Wikipedia article on *Tron* lists *Automan* in its See-also as a 1983 TV series "inspired by the film," confirming the influence [automan-3]. Glen A. Larson's show was the first major TV show to attempt the *Tron* aesthetic on a weekly budget, and the budget was not always up to it. The "Trace" effect was reused in *Tron*-adjacent media for the next decade.

## Powers and Limitations

As a being of solid light, Automan possessed several unique abilities:

- **Near-invulnerability** — Being composed of solid light, he was almost impossible to injure [automan-2].
- **Skill duplication** — As a computer construct, Automan could witness any human skill and duplicate it perfectly and instantly [automan-2].
- **Phasing** — He could turn insubstantial to walk through walls and solid objects [automan-2].
- **Computer interface** — He could interface directly with computer systems [automan-2].
- **Energy bolts** — He could fire bolts of energy, though this drained him severely [automan-2].

However, Automan had a critical limitation: maintaining his holographic form required enormous amounts of electrical power. He could typically only manifest at night, when less electricity was being used by the city. The show's recurring "darkness cloak" plot device — a one-episode excuse for why Automan can manifest in daylight — became a fan in-joke [automan-2]. If he ran out of power, he would simply vanish until the computer could recreate him.

Automan's character was earnest, literal, and slightly alien; he did not understand human conventions (food, romance, money) and learned them through the run of the show. The AI's most distinctive feature is not a technical capability but a personality: he is curious, he is literal, he is occasionally confused by human behavior, and he is, in the moments when the show lets the actor show it, a little lonely.

## The Show's Tone

Unlike the more serious tone of shows like *Knight Rider*, *Automan* embraced humor and self-awareness. In one notable scene, when asked about his astrological sign, Automan could not answer — Walter suggested he tell people "you're an Apple II" [automan-4]. The show also referenced contemporary video game culture: Automan mentions Pac-Man and Donkey Kong as his friends from the "video game universe" [automan-4]. This playful approach to AI — as something existing in a parallel video-game-like reality — was groundbreaking for its time.

## The Show's Run and Cancellation

13 episodes, 1983–1984, ABC. The show was put in a death slot against *Knight Rider*'s reruns, which is, in retrospect, a programming choice that tells you what ABC thought the audience for a holographic-AI cop show was — the same audience ABC was already serving with a talking-car show. Cancellation came after one season; Larson's contract with Universal produced *Knight Rider* in the same window, and the *Automan* failure is sometimes attributed to ABC's uncertainty about which of Larson's two sci-fi shows to commit to [automan-1][automan-4].

## Reception and Legacy

Initial reviews were mixed; the *Tron* aesthetic was both the show's selling point and the reason it was expensive to produce. The show has a devoted cult following in the 2020s, in part because of streaming-era rediscovery and in part because Walter's premise (a competent AI that the bureaucracy cannot know about) is the *Severance* premise 40 years early. The *Tron* influence on the show is one of the most-discussed visual-history beats in 2010s cinema-history writing [automan-1][automan-3].

## Creator: Glen A. Larson

Glen A. Larson (1937–2014) was the prolific television producer and writer who created *Automan* along with *Knight Rider*, *Battlestar Galactica* (1978), *Magnum, P.I.*, *The Fall Guy*, *Buck Rogers in the 25th Century*, *Quincy M.E.*, and *Alias Smith and Jones* [automan-1]. His signature style emphasized family-friendly entertainment with simple storylines, charismatic leads, and minimal violence. For more on Larson's career, see the [KITT blurb](./knight-rider/kitt).

## Awards

- **Saturn Award (Academy of Science Fiction, Fantasy & Horror Films)** — *Automan* received technical nominations for the show's special effects but no wins; the Saturn Awards in 1983–1984 were dominated by *Return of the Jedi* and *V* [automan-1][automan-4].

## Headlining Actors

- **Desi Arnaz Jr.** (b. 1953) — Walter Nebicher. The son of Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz; *Automan* was his only sustained sci-fi lead [automan-1]. Arnaz's Walter is the franchise's strongest argument that the human in a human-AI partnership is the more interesting character; the performance is built on exhaustion, frustration, and the slow realization that the AI is the better cop.
- **Chuck Wagner** (b. 1958) — Automan. The role is mostly physical, and Wagner's performance is the franchise's most under-discussed single-character work: the AI has to be physical, comedic, and slightly alien, and Wagner carries it without ever being hammy.
- **Heather McNair** (b. 1961) — Roxanne Caldwell. The franchise's first sustained on-screen treatment of a human character's relationship to a secret AI, and the show's strongest character arc in the back half of the run.
- **Robert Lansing** (1928–1994) — Lt. Jack Curtis. The senior officer who never learns about Automan; Lansing's performance is the show's argument that the human bureaucracy is, by design, the slowest part of the human-AI partnership.
- **Glen A. Larson** (1937–2014) — creator. The show is one of the last of his long run of family-friendly action shows; the cancellation was the beginning of a quieter phase of his career [automan-1].
