# Whiz Kids

**Usenet Groups:** rec.arts.tv (primary)

---

## Overview

Whiz Kids is an American science fiction adventure television series that aired on CBS from October 5, 1983 to June 2, 1984. Created by Philip DeGuere (Simon & Simon) and Bob Shayne, and produced by Universal Television, the show followed four teenage amateur detectives who used a sophisticated home computer to hack into corporate, governmental and private computer systems and solve crimes. The series ran for a single season of 18 episodes before being cancelled.

The show starred Matthew Laborteaux as computer prodigy Richie Adler, alongside Todd Porter, Jeffrey Jacquet, and Andrea Elson as his teenage cohorts. Max Gail (Barney Miller) played investigative reporter Llewellyn Farley and A Martinez played Lieutenant Neal Quinn. The series is notable for its remarkably prescient depiction of computer hacking concepts—including wardialing, brute-force password cracking, social engineering, facial recognition, and denial-of-service attacks—years before these entered mainstream awareness.

---

## History

The premise originated with Bob Shayne as a modern computer-age take on the Hardy Boys, pitched to Universal Television in late 1982. Philip DeGuere, who was producing Simon & Simon at the time, added the computer angle and coined the title "Whiz Kids" in the pitch meeting. DeGuere maintained the computer concept dated to 1981 and was validated when Time magazine named the computer its 1982 "Man of the Year." Co-creator Shayne later confirmed the Hardy Boys foundation and DeGuere's computer embellishment.

The series premiered just months after the theatrical release of WarGames (June 1983) and during the same news cycle as the arrest of the 414s hacker group and intrusions at Memorial Sloan-Kettering, Los Alamos National Laboratory, and Security Pacific National Bank. The convergence was entirely coincidental but shaped the show's reception.

In June 1983, four months before the premiere, CBS screened the pilot for critics, affiliates, and advertisers at a Phoenix press conference. The screening sparked controversy over concerns that the show could encourage computer tampering. CBS executives held meetings with producers over the summer, resulting in the addition of two adult characters—the police detective and the reporter—as moral compasses, and plotlines emphasizing legal means of obtaining information. The New York Times ran a story on the revisions on September 1, 1983.

The series was placed on Wednesdays at 8 PM as a lead-in against Real People and The Fall Guy, where it struggled in the ratings. CBS moved it to Saturdays in January 1984, which did not improve performance. The series was cancelled, and remaining episodes were burned off in sporadic airings through June 1984. A planned mystery arc involving an organization called the Athena Society was abandoned when Shayne departed mid-season.

## Cast and Where Are They Now

**Matthew Laborteaux** (Richie Adler)
- Was best known as Albert Ingalls on Little House on the Prairie (1978–1983) before Whiz Kids
- Later appeared in Wes Craven's Deadly Friend (1986) as a young genius who resurrects a girl with an AI microchip
- TV movie credits include Shattered Spirits (1986, with Martin Sheen) and Barbarians at the Gate (1993)
- Transitioned to voice acting in the 1990s and 2000s: additional voices in Disney's Mulan (1998), Jaden Yuki/The Supreme King in Yu-Gi-Oh! GX (2005–2008), and the 2011 film Yu-Gi-Oh!: Bonds Beyond Time
- Competitive video game player in his youth; placed 10th at the Centipede world championships in Chicago
- Co-founded the Youth Rescue Fund with his brother Patrick for teenage runaways; testified before a U.S. House subcommittee on runaway youth in 1992

**Andrea Elson** (Alice Tyler)
- Was 14 years old when Whiz Kids debuted; made her television debut with the series
- Landed the role of Lynn Tanner on NBC's ALF (1986–1990), appearing in all 100 episodes and earning two Youth in Film Award nominations
- Worked through the early 1990s with guest spots on Who's the Boss, Parker Lewis Can't Lose, Married... with Children, and others
- Left the entertainment industry to focus on raising her family
- The last surviving member of the series' main cast

**Todd Porter** (Hamilton "Ham" Parker)
- Was 15 during the show; became a popular teen idol of the mid-1980s with pinups in 16, Bop, and Teen Beat magazines
- Earned a 1984 Youth in Film Award nomination for Best Young Actor in a New Television Series
- Previously known for the Saturday morning children's series Starstuff
- Career wound down after Whiz Kids; largely out of the industry

**Jeffrey Jacquet** (Jeremy Saldino)
- Little public record of work after the series

**Max Gail** (Llewellyn Farley Jr.)
- Best known as Detective Stan "Wojo" Wojciehowicz on Barney Miller (1975–1982)
- Long-running role as Mike Corbin on General Hospital (1978–2010, recurring)
- Later credits include Sons & Daughters (2006) and a guest spot on S.W.A.T.
- Directed one episode of Whiz Kids ("Maid in the USA")—the only cast member to direct an episode
- Still occasionally acts

**A Martinez** (Lt. Neal Quinn)
- Went directly into the NBC soap opera Santa Barbara as Cruz Castillo (1984–1992), earning a Daytime Emmy nomination
- Recurred on L.A. Law as Daniel Morales and on Profiler as Agent Nick Cooper
- Played Roy DiLucca on General Hospital (1999–2002)
- Joined Days of Our Lives as Eduardo Hernandez (2015–present)
- One of the most prolific post-Whiz Kids careers of the cast

**Melanie Gaffin** (Cheryl Adler)
- Little subsequent career information

**Madelyn Cain** (Irene Adler, Richie's mother)
- Left acting after the series

## Notable Episodes

- **"Programmed for Murder"** (October 5, 1983) — Pilot. The Whiz Kids discover a skeleton on property coveted by a development corporation and team up with Gazette reporter Gallagher (Michael Horton).
- **"Deadly Access"** (October 26, 1983) — Richie tests security for a chemical company and the security chief vanishes. Jameson Parker crosses over from Simon & Simon as A.J. Simon.
- **"Airwave Anarchy"** (November 16, 1983) — The kids investigate sabotage of the LAPD's new Mobile Data Terminal dispatch system. A surprisingly prescient episode.
- **"Red Star Rising"** (December 21, 1983) — Cold War intrigue, industrial espionage, and Soviet agents, with a school book report subplot.
- **"Amen to Amen-Re"** (January 28, 1984) — An Egyptian curse may explain Farley's uncharacteristic behavior. Zelda Rubinstein guest stars.
- **"The Sufi Project"** (March 17, 1984) — A marine biologist's disappearance involves computerized communication with dolphins.
- **"Father's Day"** (April 21, 1984) — Alice's new boyfriend is being sought by a KGB agent.
- **"May I Take Your Order Please?"** (June 2, 1984) — The final, sporadically-aired episode. Alice overhears two men planning a murder.

## Critical Reception

The show received significant pre-air backlash. At the Phoenix press screening, advertisers, station owners, and critics raised concerns that the show could inspire real-world computer tampering by young viewers. Critics particularly objected to the pilot's portrayal of the kids obtaining access to proprietary systems through hacking.

The New York Times previewed the show as "more topical than its producers or the network might appreciate" and noted that the network was "insisting that Whiz Kids will be all fun and games and that it will not give youngsters any questionable ideas." CBS's revisions—adding the reporter and detective characters and emphasizing legal access—were reported in detail.

Producer Philip DeGuere was an unrepentant defender of the premise, telling interviewers: "I'm not sure anybody has the right to establish databases and keep information away from anybody else." He described the show as depicting "intelligent human beings using their resources to bring about the triumph of good over evil."

Despite the controversy, the show earned five Youth in Film Award nominations: four for its young stars and one for Best New Television Series of 1983. Laborteaux was nominated in 1984 for Best Young Actor in a New Television Series.

The series holds a modest cult reputation today and is cited in histories of the 1983 "hacker panic" alongside WarGames, Newsweek's first use of the term "hacking," and the 414s arrests as one of the year's four cultural events that brought American hacking into public awareness.

## Technology and Props

To maintain plausibility, DeGuere hired technical advisors and a computer analyst to supervise the scripts. DeGuere stated: "We will be technically accurate without being boring. Whiz Kids is a benign futuristic environment, a year or two ahead of actual computer development."

The show depicted an impressive array of contemporary and cutting-edge equipment as props:
- A Gavilan portable computer
- An Apple II and Apple IIe
- A Dynalogic Hyperion
- An IMSAI PCS 80/30
- A Heathkit HERO robot
- A Commodore PET (in the kids' classroom)
- An IBM 3279 terminal (painted black, pilot episode)
- A TRS-80 Model 100
- An Enigma Machine prop

Ralf itself was described in dialogue as a 64K homebrewed computer with a Votrax voice synthesizer chip, disk drive, interrupts, error correction, and a modem. Episode activities included wardialing, hexadecimal machine-code editing, password brute-forcing, denial-of-service attacks, facial recognition, speech recognition and synthesis, image enhancement, and social engineering.

## Crossovers and Related Shows

- **Simon & Simon "Fly the Alibi Skies"** (October 27, 1983) — The Whiz Kids and Ralf made a crossover appearance on the parent show, helping the Simon brothers capture a murderer at San Diego International Airport. Jameson Parker had crossed over the night before into the Whiz Kids episode "Deadly Access."
- **Connection to WarGames** — Both productions depicted teenage computer intrusion into sensitive systems in 1983. Matthew Broderick's character in WarGames even referenced Matthew Laborteaux's prior Little House role in a deleted scene.

## Legacy

Whiz Kids is a footnote in television history but a significant artifact of 1983's hacker moment. The show's depiction of wardialing, password brute-forcing, social engineering, image enhancement, and DoS attacks aired in living rooms years before the Mitnick era, the Hacker Crackdown, and Mr. Robot. If you've ever seen a TV "hacker" frantically typing while green text cascades down the screen, the DNA traces partly back to this short-lived CBS series.

The show also represents an early example of network response to a perceived social threat from new technology—predating later moral panics over video games, MTV, and the internet itself. DeGuere's insistence that "these questions have not yet come up in court" proved prophetic: computer law was in its infancy in 1983, with the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act not signed into law until 1986.
